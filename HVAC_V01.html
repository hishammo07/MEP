Equal Friction Method

The equal friction method for sizing air ducts is easy and straightforward to use.

Method summary

  1. Compute the necessary air volume flow (m³/h, cfm) in every room and branch of the system
  2. Use step 1 to compute the total air volume (m³/h, cfm) in the main system
  3. Determine the maximum acceptable airflow velocity in the main duct
  4. Determine the friction (major) pressure drop per unit length in the main duct
  5. Use that friction rate as a constant to determine the duct sizes throughout the distribution system
  6. Determine the total resistance of the duct system: friction rate × equivalent length of the longest run (plus fitting losses)
  7. Compute the balancing dampers

1. Compute the air volume in every room and branch

Use the actual heat, cooling or air quality requirements for the rooms and calculate the required air volume flow q.

2. Compute the total volume flow in the system

Make a simplified diagram of the system like the one above and accumulate the flows to get the total qtotal.

Note! Maximum load conditions almost never occur in all rooms at the same time. Avoid over-sizing the main system by multiplying the accumulated volume by a factor less than one (the diversity factor below). This is probably the hard part, and for larger systems computer-assisted indoor climate calculations are often required.

3. Determine the maximum acceptable airflow velocity in the main ducts

Determine the maximum velocity in the main ducts from the application environment. To avoid disturbing noise, keep velocities within these limits:

  • comfort systems – air velocity 4 to 7 m/s (13 to 23 ft/s)
  • industrial systems – air velocity 8 to 12 m/s (26 to 40 ft/s)
  • high speed systems – air velocity 10 to 18 m/s (33 to 60 ft/s)

Use the maximum velocity limit when selecting the size of the main duct.

4. Determine the friction pressure drop in the main duct

Use a friction loss table or chart (or the calculator below) to find the pressure drop per unit length in the main duct.

5. Determine the duct sizes throughout the system

Use the friction rate from step 4 as a constant. Use the air volumes from step 1 and select the duct sizes whose friction rate is as close to the main duct value as possible.

6. Determine the total resistance in the system

Use the friction rate from step 4 to calculate the pressure drop through the longest part of the duct system. Use the equivalent length, which is the actual length plus additional lengths for bends, T's, inlets and outlets. The calculator below uses minor loss coefficients (K) for fittings instead.

7. Calculate balancing dampers

Use the total resistance from step 6 and the volume flow throughout the system to calculate the necessary dampers and the theoretical pressure loss through them.

Note about the Equal Friction Method

The equal friction method is straightforward and easy to use and gives an automatic reduction of air velocities through the system. The reduced velocities are generally within the noise limits of the application environment.

The method can require more duct size reductions than other methods, and the often poorer pressure balance requires more adjusting dampers. This may increase the system cost compared to other methods.

Reference

Key equations

  • Flow: q = A · v
  • Dynamic (velocity) pressure: pd = ρ v² / 2 (Pa, v in m/s, ρ in kg/m³)
  • Friction loss (Darcy–Weisbach): Δp/L = f · (ρ v² / 2) / D
  • Friction factor (Swamee–Jain): f = 0.25 / [log₁₀(ε/(3.7D) + 5.74/Re⁰·⁹)]²; laminar f = 64/Re
  • Reynolds number: Re = v D / ν
  • Fitting loss: Δp = K · ρ v² / 2; equivalent length Le = K · D / f
  • Rectangular equivalent diameter: Dₑ = 1.3 (ab)^0.625 / (a+b)^0.25
  • Flat oval equivalent diameter: Dₑ = 1.55 A^0.625 / P^0.25, A = πb²/4 + b(a−b), P = πb + 2(a−b)
  • Total pressure: pt = ps + pd
  • Static regain: Δpregain = R · ρ/2 · (v₁² − v₂²)
  • Sensible heat: q = Q / (ρ · cp · ΔT)
  • Air power: P = q · Δp; shaft power = P / ηfan; SFP = electrical power / q (kW per m³/s)
  • Damper loss: Δpdamper = index path loss − path loss; K = Δp / pd

Glossary

  • Index (critical) path: the path with the highest total resistance. It sets the fan pressure.
  • Major loss: friction in straight duct. Minor loss: losses in fittings.
  • Diversity factor: allowance that not all spaces peak at once.
  • Aspect ratio: duct width ÷ height. Keep it at or below about 4, and ideally below 3.
  • Balancing damper: adds resistance to the shorter paths so every outlet receives design flow.
  • Static regain: static pressure recovered when velocity falls downstream of a branch.

Design checklist

  1. Calculate room flows from loads and ventilation requirements.
  2. Pick velocity limits for the application and noise criteria.
  3. Size the main duct and fix the design friction rate.
  4. Size all sections; keep aspect ratios sensible.
  5. Count all fittings and use turning vanes in mitered elbows.
  6. Find the index path and add equipment losses (filters, coils, silencers, terminals).
  7. Size balancing dampers; avoid large damper losses (they cause noise).
  8. Select the fan at design flow plus leakage allowance and the total system pressure.
  9. Check noise, insulation, access doors and fire/smoke damper requirements.
